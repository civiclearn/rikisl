Filipinos in Iceland: a growing community

Filipinos are one of the fastest-growing immigrant communities in Iceland. Filipino women make up about 5% of all female immigrants, and Filipinos were the second-largest group receiving Icelandic citizenship in 2024 (37 grants). Many Filipinos in Iceland work in healthcare, hospitality, and food processing — sectors with strong job security and long-term prospects.

As a Filipino, you bring a significant advantage to learning Icelandic: strong English skills. Here's how to leverage that, and what challenges to prepare for.

Using English as your bridge to Icelandic

English and Icelandic are both Germanic languages. They share a common ancestor, which means you'll find surprising overlaps:

  • Common vocabulary roots — "father" = faðir, "mother" = móðir, "house" = hús, "book" = bók, "come" = koma, "water" = vatn. Many basic words are recognisable.
  • Sentence structure — both languages use Subject-Verb-Object as a default order. The Icelandic V2 rule adds a twist, but the base pattern is familiar.
  • The Latin alphabet — Icelandic uses the same alphabet as English, plus a few extra letters (ð, þ, æ, ö). No new script to learn.

Challenges Filipino speakers face

Grammatical gender and cases

Filipino/Tagalog has no grammatical gender and no case system. Icelandic has three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter) and four cases. This will be the biggest adjustment. Every noun, adjective, and pronoun changes form depending on its role in the sentence. At A2 level, you need to get the basics right — not perfection, but enough to be understood.

Pronunciation

Filipino pronunciation is quite different from Icelandic. Key challenges include:

  • The "r" sound — Icelandic uses a rolled/trilled R, which Filipino speakers can usually learn (it's similar to the Spanish R common in Tagalog loanwords).
  • ð and þ — the "th" sounds. These exist in English, so practise them in English first, then transfer to Icelandic words.
  • Vowel distinctions — Icelandic has more vowel sounds than Filipino. Pay special attention to ö, æ, and the difference between short and long vowels.

Cold-weather vocabulary

Icelandic has an extensive vocabulary for weather, landscape, and seasons that may be completely unfamiliar. Words like "snjór" (snow), "hálka" (ice on roads), "norðanátt" (north wind) come up frequently in daily life and in exam texts.

Study strategies for Filipino speakers

  • Start with English-Icelandic resources — many Icelandic textbooks and online courses are designed for English speakers. Use these rather than trying to find Filipino-Icelandic materials (which barely exist).
  • Watch Icelandic TV with English subtitles first, then switch to Icelandic subtitles as you progress. RÚV (the national broadcaster) offers free streaming.
  • Focus on the case system early — it will feel unnatural at first, but it's the foundation of Icelandic grammar. Even basic A2 conversations require you to use cases correctly.
  • Connect with the Filipino community — there are Filipino groups in the Reykjavík area who organise study circles and share tips. Facebook groups are a good starting point.
  • Practise writing daily — even a short Icelandic text (a grocery list, a note to a colleague) builds muscle memory for grammar patterns.
Tip: Your English is your superpower. Most Icelandic learning resources are in English, and the vocabulary overlap between English and Icelandic gives you a head start that speakers of non-Germanic languages don't have.
