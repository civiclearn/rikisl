Eastern European immigrants in Iceland

After Poland, the largest European immigrant groups in Iceland come from Lithuania (about 5–6% of immigrants), Romania (about 5–6%), and a mix of other EU/EEA countries. Together with Poles, Eastern Europeans make up roughly half of all immigrants in Iceland.

If you speak Lithuanian, Romanian, Czech, Slovak, Latvian, Hungarian, Bulgarian, or Croatian, this guide covers the shared challenges and advantages you have when learning Icelandic for the ríkisborgarapróf.

Shared advantages

Grammatical complexity is familiar

Most Eastern European languages have features that make Icelandic grammar less alien:

  • Lithuanian — has 7 cases, grammatical gender, and complex verb conjugation. Icelandic's 4 cases and 3 genders will feel like a lighter version of what you already know.
  • Romanian — has grammatical gender, a case system (albeit simpler), and definite articles that attach to the noun (similar to Icelandic's suffixed article).
  • Czech/Slovak — 7 cases, 3 genders, extensive declension. Very close to the grammatical mindset needed for Icelandic.

The key insight: you already think in a language with cases and gender. You don't need to learn these concepts — just their Icelandic implementation.

EEA freedom of movement

As EU/EEA citizens, Lithuanians and Romanians can live and work in Iceland without a residence permit. This makes it easy to establish the continuous residency required for citizenship. You don't face the administrative hurdles that non-EEA nationals do.

Shared challenges

Vocabulary starts from zero

Icelandic is a North Germanic language. It shares almost no vocabulary with Lithuanian (Baltic), Romanian (Romance), or other Eastern European languages. Every word must be learned fresh. This is the biggest time investment.

Pronunciation

Icelandic has several sounds that don't exist in Eastern European languages:

  • ð and þ — the "th" sounds (soft and hard). These are rare in European languages outside English and Icelandic.
  • Aspirated stops — Icelandic p, t, k are heavily aspirated (breathy) at the start of words, more so than in most Eastern European languages.
  • Vowel length — the distinction between short and long vowels changes word meaning in Icelandic, which is not a feature of most Eastern European languages.

Limited learning resources in your language

Unlike Polish (which has a substantial community and some Icelandic learning resources), Lithuanian and Romanian speakers have very few dedicated Icelandic materials. You will almost certainly need to learn Icelandic through English — so a working knowledge of English is effectively a prerequisite.

Practical strategies

  • Learn through English — use English-language Icelandic courses like Icelandic Online, and English-Icelandic textbooks. Your English doesn't need to be perfect — intermediate is sufficient.
  • Attend the grunnnám í íslensku — the subsidised 240-hour programme. Classes are multilingual and taught in simple Icelandic, so your native language doesn't matter.
  • Use your grammatical intuition — when studying Icelandic cases, map them to your native language's cases. The nominative, accusative, dative, and genitive categories work similarly.
  • Focus on the exam format — the ríkisborgarapróf has a specific format. Practising with realistic simulations is more efficient than general Icelandic study once you're at A2 level.
  • Build a daily routine — 30 minutes of focused practice every day is more effective than 3 hours once a week. Use commute time for listening practice.
Your grammatical background is your biggest asset. While vocabulary is a grind for everyone, the structural patterns of Icelandic — cases, gender, conjugation — will click faster for you than for speakers of languages without these features.
