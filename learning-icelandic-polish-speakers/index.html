Polish speakers and the ríkisborgarapróf

Polish citizens are by far the largest immigrant group in Iceland — over 22,900 residents as of January 2025, making up 31% of all immigrants. Polish nationals also consistently receive the most citizenship grants each year (110 in 2024, 156 in 2023). If you are Polish and living in Iceland, the ríkisborgarapróf is almost certainly in your future.

The good news: learning Icelandic from Polish is challenging but very achievable at A2 level. Here's what to expect and how to prepare strategically.

Advantages Polish speakers have

  • Case system familiarity — Polish has 7 grammatical cases; Icelandic has 4 (nominative, accusative, dative, genitive). You already understand the concept of declining nouns, which is one of the biggest hurdles for speakers of languages without cases.
  • Consonant clusters — Polish trains your mouth for complex consonant combinations. Icelandic clusters like "fljótt" or "strætó" will feel less intimidating.
  • Work ethic and community — the Polish community in Iceland is large and well-established. There are Polish-Icelandic social groups, churches, and networks where you can find study partners.

Common challenges

Pronunciation

Icelandic has sounds that don't exist in Polish. The most difficult for Polish speakers tend to be:

  • ð (eð) — a soft "th" sound (as in English "the"). Polish has no equivalent.
  • þ (þorn) — a hard "th" sound (as in English "think"). Also absent from Polish.
  • Double L (ll) — pronounced as a "tl" or "dl" sound, unlike anything in Polish.
  • Vowel length — Icelandic distinguishes short and long vowels. Polish vowels are relatively uniform in length.

Word order

Icelandic word order is more flexible than English but has strict rules about verb placement (V2 rule — the verb must be the second element). Polish is very flexible with word order, so you may need to develop a new instinct for where the verb goes.

Vocabulary

There is almost zero overlap between Polish and Icelandic vocabulary. Unlike learning German or English (which share some roots with Icelandic), Polish speakers start from scratch with nearly every word. This makes vocabulary building the single most time-intensive part of your preparation.

Study strategies for Polish speakers

  • Start with the 240-hour programme — attend the grunnnám í íslensku at your nearest adult education centre. The classes are designed for immigrants and are heavily subsidised.
  • Use Icelandic Online — the free University of Iceland course at icelandiconline.com. It's structured by CEFR level and covers all skills.
  • Build vocabulary with daily exposure — label items in your home in Icelandic, change your phone language, listen to RÚV radio during your commute.
  • Practise speaking at work — many Poles in Iceland work in construction, fishing, or tourism where Icelandic is used daily. Ask colleagues to speak Icelandic with you, even when it's easier to switch to English or Polish.
  • Join a tungumálakaffi — these free language cafés are held in libraries and community centres across Iceland. They're a low-pressure way to practise conversation.
  • Practise all four exam sections equally — Polish speakers often do well on reading and writing but underestimate listening and speaking. The exam weights all sections equally.
